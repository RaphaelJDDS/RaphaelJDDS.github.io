[PROJECT] Designing an induction radial-flow BLDC motor

Published on 1/21/2025, 10:53 Pm (GMT).

  Initially, "Learning the basics of Microcontrollers, Assembly programming, PWM, circuitry and PCB design, part development and induction motor operation in order to design one of the latter." seemed like an appropriate title. But that would've been too long. This page is long as well; Therefore, if you want to skip the details, visit this folder.

 This article - for the sake of organization - is hence divided into broad operational sections. As a side note, this project's been a literal nightmare, with many obstacles along the way. Enjoy reading me trying to explain it.

 

  System architecture

 

  A nice way to introduce the chosen architecture is by examining how an induction motor works in principle , and building up requirements from there. Basically, the device functions by rotating a magnetic field around a freely-rotating conductor. How does that possibly spin the conductor ? Simple. The change in magnetic field will trigger a current inside the core conductor (i.e. the rotor), such that it creates an opposing magnetic field. Or, you may want to use the right-hand rule, and you'll find that the force will appear tangent to the rotor, triggering the rotation, as it is perpendicular to the current (flowing length-wise, or axially) and the magnetic flux (radial), as [F] = I*[L]x[B].

 Now that that's settled, how is it possible to create a rotating magnetic field without using fancy sensors and ICs to trigger electromagnets? That's a little less straightforward. Essentially: If you wind up three coils of wire around a core, and evenly space them along a certain circumference, and pass three AC signals with phases offset by 2π/3, the fields induced by the current add up and cancel out into a single, rotating magnetic field.

 Now, all that is left is figure out how to, from an DC signal, generate the three phase-offset AC signals. That's when a little creativity is needed. As for how, I went with programming a PWM into a PIC10F200 IC, and using the CD4017SA IC as a three-way phase splitter. PWM stands for Pulse-Width modulation, and what it does is vary the length of constant-voltage pulses, so that they smoothen out to a sinusoid by passing them thorugh capacitors (for current) and inductors. And that's basically it as for system architecture.

 

  Circuit design

 

  As shown in the system architecture section, two ICs were chosen, and a voltage of 5V was required. The only problem was that I would've overrun current limits had I used the AC directly from the phase splitter to drive the coils, thus requiring a push-pull amplification transistor arrangement as shown:

Schematic for the circuit, note how the FSZT ICs are NPN, whilst the ZTXs are PNP.


 Other than the addition of a 100nF decoupling capacitor (for overcurrent/EMI prevention), there isn't much remarkable to talk about on this front. Note that "Induction coil" is a mere placename for where the (generic) spools would be connected/soldered.

 

  PCB design

 

  On the matter of PCBs, are generally most noteworthy three main objectives: Layer count minimization (preferrably down to two), trace length reduction/decoupling capacitor usage (preventing EMI/EMP) and trace thickness maximization(reducing resistance, particulatly in power-carrying cases). In this case, the circuit is fairly simple, and thus all such requirements can be easily dealt with given the wide tolerances (e.g. the virtual irrelevance of AC-signal noise). Still, distributing the elements was a demanding task, as that determines the required trace complexity.

  I also took the care to minimize board size, whilst trying to avoid cramping elements in order to simplify soldering. The compromises I made, along with my distinct lack of expertise, resulted in the following board:

As you can see, some traces are thicker than others, as power-transmission ones were in need of lower resistances (in order to maximize current) than their merely AC signal-carrying counterparts



 

  Programming the PIC MCU

 

 This has to be one of the coolest parts. Remember the PWM? The microcontroller has to deliver a varying electrical duty cycle, which in code is synonymous with varying delays to switch an output (e.g. GP1/General-purpose 1 pin) on (+5V/VCC) and off (GND).

  As for implementation, the main hurdle comes down to writing in assembly, a very low-level language, meaning "time" is not in-built, and so variable-length instruction loops are to be introduced. The final solution achieved can be found here. Remember the code was written to be compiled by MPASM (only available for MPLAB v5.35 or earlier).

Assembly language is pretty technical, and unfortunately commenting doesn't make the mnemonics and logic justice.



  As for configuring the PIC, you can connect it to a PICKIT (any model) and connect the latter to a computer running the MPLAB IDE (by Microchip), where the code should be compiled.

 

  Designing the mechanical components

 

  This was the last section of my development process. Essentially, there are three main mechanical parts in the device, a stator (which stays in place, with the windings), a rotor (which rotates) and a roller bearing (to attach the rotor while allowing it to spin).

 The stator, hence, has to provide attachments for the windings, and be cilindrical. The design I ended up with was the following:



  Other than the plastic atatchments to the bearing, it's a pretty plain design. Remember the copper windings are to be 120 degrees apart.

Meanwhile, the roller bearings are fairly standard, also being possible to make them out of plastic:

The other side has the fittings for the stator's attachments. It is closed by the rotor, which will soon be shown.



  The rotor, being where currents are induced, has to be electrically conductive, and has those serrated edges to prevent eddy current-related loss. It is extremely important that it is not made from a single hunk of material, as the 3D model may imply, and instead out of compressed anodized (or just mutually insulated) sheets of conductive material (typically Silicon Steel), as that reduces axial eddy current losses.

The large clamp at the non-serrated side is the closure for the roller bearing. Not needed if one intends to buy a premade bearing, but that's literally a trick in order to simplify the final assembly.
