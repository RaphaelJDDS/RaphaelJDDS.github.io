[PROJECT] Developing an integrated MP3 player

Published on 8/2/2025, 3:29 Pm (GMT).

  Initially, I genuinely thought this would be a simple project; I was really wrong. It ended up pushing the envelope of my abilities, from EDA to Blender, at a surprising scale - and I am glad to have finally, actually finished it.

  This page is a summary of the development of a crude MP3 player with a screen GUI (that only interprets .wav files). If you wish to avoid the details, you can visit this folder and watch the following CGI demo I've made in blender.

System architecture

  Essentially, the player is a fairly simple type of device, centered around a principle of portability and simplicity of design.
 The actual design mostly revolved around a CPU, a STM32H7VET6 chip (hereinafter referred to as "STM32"), specifically selected for its sufficient processing power for DAC and GUI rendering, as well as for the whole STMicro 'ecosystem' around it. The board, thus, requires a screen (for displaying the GUI), a battery, a means of charging said battery, a TRS audio jack (for outputting the actual signal), buttons for selection, and a MicroSD card slot for storage. Since I've taken a "first principles" look at it, some design choices may appear to be rather strange.

 As with the power side of things, I decided to integrate a AAA battery holder and hook it up to a USB-C Li-ion charging/discharging module (the TP4056), with the intention of inserting a rechargeable battery there. The only issue was that the module outputs 5V, well above the 3.3V required or supported by all components. This was solved by introducing a buck converter IC, responsible for converting between these two voltages (with a current-boosted, load-adjusted output).

 The display required a little more consideration, though, especially when choosing which interfacing protocol would be best. I ended up going with SPI, given its compatibility with the ST7735 library, used by the STM32 to drive the screen.

 

  CPU programming

 This was by far the longest and most draining part of the process. Essentially, what had to be implemented were a SDMMC interface (for reading data from the MicroSD card), Digital to Analog Conversion (DAC), and a functioning (albeit crude) GUI controlled by pull-up buttons. My greatest hurdles were in a lack of familiarity relative to the STM32 IDE and with the functioning of an MCU larger than a small PIC.

 My main objective was to simplify the code as much as possible, thus I employed the fatfs library for the SDMMC interface, the STM7735 library for the GUI, and the in-built DAC implementation of the IDE. The GUI took the most time, as all the logic beyond its library had to be hand-coded, more so than with the fatfs counterpart.

  Circuit design

  The circuit design process was fairly basic for this project, though also full of new requirements (though luckily, the low signal frequencies made impedance matching unnecessary).

  Here, the main points worthy of mention are the pull-up resistors (for buttons), which aren't particularly complicated but were surely new to me, the buck converter, also simple in implementation despite novelty, the power management and proper decoupling for signal integrity (fairly complex).

  Specifically, the board consisted of four planes, one for Ground (GND), another for the Common Collector (VCC), and an analog counterpart for each, with the latter being connected to the former only once and through a resistor, for conserving the integrity of the separate signal types (and avoiding noisy power traces from interfering in the analog output). Decoupling capacitors of various capacitances had, thus, to be employed to avoid Electromagnetic Interference of various frequencies.

  PCB design

  Apart from the programming, the PCB design was the most difficult step to accomplish. Luckily, there weren't as many rigorous requirements and components as in some other boards. Proper routing was a novelty, though it pales in comparison to plane management and appropriate component placement.

 As for specific sections of the board, there is much to mention. The power management was mostly segregated to its own region in the board due to noise concerns, and required a fair bit of creativity, as shown in the System Architecture section. It was impossible to completely segregate it due to other requirements (namely that the USB-C, TRS and MicroSD connectors should be nearby). Analog signals were sent preferrably within the lower, analog planes of the circuit in avoidance of interference.

  Traces and vias were sized to their relative current flows, as to minimize resistances and board area (managing their trade-offs); and decoupling capacitors were placed near highly susceptible components, namely the CPU+Connectors region.
